Magnitude

Which world problems deserve more effort?

Before a problem deserves more effort, it has to be SHARPS: wide in Scope, deep in Harm, Accelerating, with a big Response gap, Probable, and Solvable. Six plain questions turn any problem, from a factory closing to AI loss of control, into one score you can compare, defend and cite.

real-world problems placed by people affected per year. Each step along the line is ten times more people. Swipe sideways to see the whole scale.

Count the zeros

World problems differ by enormous amounts. A family dispute touches ten people; malaria touches 263 million. On an ordinary 1–10 scale those would be squashed together, so the rubric scores the number of zeros instead. That is a logarithmic scale, the same idea as decibels or the Richter scale.

Because every point means ten times more, adding scores is the same as multiplying the real numbers. That is how one simple sum can respect the multiplication at the heart of effective altruism's ITN framework (importance × tractability × neglectedness).

Why the two rankings differ

    What this rubric cannot score

    • Factory farming (80,000 Hours #6) can now be scored, but only if you turn on "Animals" in Settings, because it needs a contested "moral weight" for each species. Wild animal suffering (#7) is not scored: the numbers of wild animals and the quality of their lives are too uncertain for this rubric.
    • Extreme power concentration (#2) and newer priorities such as the moral status of digital minds. Their harm is mostly about the long-term future and power, not healthy life lost per year, so the inputs here do not fit well.
    • This is a real limitation worth naming: by default, a method built on human DALYs quietly decides that animals and future digital minds do not count. The Animals setting makes that choice visible instead of hidden.

    Why the SHARPS maths works

    Each point names the idea behind it, so you can defend the method in a paper or a discussion.

    The pain scale

    Harm is not just time: it is time × how bad it feels, from 0 (fine) to 1 (as bad as death). Each level below says what it feels like, what the person can still do, and gives real conditions as anchors. A week of bad flu (0.05) counts as about eight hours of healthy life lost; a year of severe depression (about 0.66) counts as about eight months. The weights roughly follow the Global Burden of Disease, which built them from large surveys where people compared health states.

    Two critiques worth naming: survey weights may undercount intense and mental suffering, which a wellbeing measure (the WELLBY) would rate higher; but people who live with a disability often rate their lives better than outsiders predict.

    Weights are approximate values from the Global Burden of Disease disability weights; exact figures vary slightly between editions. Between two levels? Type your own number, e.g. 0.08.

    Edit it into your own words before using it.

    In your paper

    • Problem selection: the score and range justify why your problem matters.
    • Evidence: every score traces back to a cited number.
    • Perspectives: each setting is a lens. The discount rate is the Stern vs Nordhaus climate debate.
    • Robustness: report whether your ranking survives the robustness check.
    • Limitations: name two or three from below.

    Limitations to name

    • Aggregation. Adding harm across people is a utilitarian assumption: many small harms can outweigh one big one. It is why mild harms spread over billions, like energy poverty or learning poverty, can outrank deadlier but narrower problems like HIV/AIDS. Rights and fairness views disagree; the Subgroups option shows where the harm is concentrated.
    • What it misses. Healthy life lost barely registers culture, dignity or nature for its own sake.
    • Judgment calls. Response gap and Solvability are judgments, and the maths treats an ordinal scale as if each step were exactly 3×.
    • Risky tails. Adding best guesses undercounts rare worst cases (Jensen's inequality), so uncertain risks such as nuclear war are somewhat understated.
    • Value settings. The discount rate and money exchange rate are value judgments; they can reorder long-term problems.
    • Shape of change. It assumes steady growth, then no change after the forecast window.
    • Future generations. After a one-time catastrophe, the loss of everyone never born is not scored. Longtermists call this the largest harm of all.
    • Marginal, not total. It scores the value of more effort, not how bad a problem is. A terrible but well-funded problem can rank low.
